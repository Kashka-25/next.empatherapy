# Business model research: how comparable platforms earn money

Prepared 13 Nov 2026 for Empatherapy. This reports what comparable businesses do. It is not financial, tax or legal advice. Most pricing came from search summaries of third-party pages, so "checked" means seen on 13 Nov 2026 via search or fetch, not verified against a live rate card. Items I could not confirm are marked UNSURE.

## (a) Summary: most decision-relevant findings

1. Directories for therapists mostly charge practitioners a flat listing fee, not commission. Australian counsellor directories reported about AUD 154 ex GST per year (GoodTherapy AU, https://www.goodtherapy.com.au/flex/register/363/1) and AUD 198 per year (Talking Works, via search, https://www.talkingworks.wordpress.zeald.com/?p=40252). Psychology Today is reported at about USD 29.95 per month (secondary sources only, https://www.choosingtherapy.com/best-therapist-directories/).
2. Commission-style marketplaces that compare best to your idea charge only on the first booking from a new client. Mindbody takes 20% of a new client's first purchase, capped at USD 30 (https://www.mindbodyonline.com/business/education/blog/what-does-a-new-client-from-the-mindbody-app-cost-in-2026). Fresha reportedly takes 20% on new marketplace clients with a minimum (see table caveat), and no fee on returning clients (https://www.fresha.com/pricing).
3. Zocdoc charges per new-patient booking, reported at USD 30 to 140 depending on specialty and location (https://www.zocdoc.com/blog/pay-per-booking-fees-explained/ via search). Practitioners objected when it was introduced (https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/practices/some-new-york-doctors-unhappy-about-zocdoc-s-new-pricing-model-company-says-it-was).
4. Minimum fees bite at low prices. One analysis of Fresha shows a USD 6 minimum turns a 20% fee into 30% on a USD 20 service (https://pabau.com/blog/fresha-pricing/). At AUD 60 to 200 sessions, a percentage fee is about AUD 12 to 40.
5. Leakage research is mixed. One study found commissions roughly doubled detected leakage (about 4 percentage points; https://english.phbs.pku.edu.cn/info/3882/48882.htm). Another paper argues leakage in a home-cleaning platform was small (https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/bray/doc/disintermediation/disintermediation.pdf). Practitioner guides say value-adds beat contact-hiding, but with little hard data.
6. Because Empatherapy has no on-platform booking or payment, a commission cannot be verified or collected without building payments. A flat fee, or a fee on enquiry, can be collected without payments. Enquiry-based charging risks a "pay per lead" reputation.
7. AHPRA: testimonials in advertising a regulated health service are prohibited, and a booking or review platform that advertises such services is responsible for its own review function (https://www.ahpra.gov.au/Resources/Advertising-hub/Resources-for-advertisers/Testimonial-tool.aspx). Reviews on Empatherapy are a real compliance question for registered practitioners.
8. I could NOT find the exact wording on paying or receiving referral fees in the Psychology Board or shared National Board codes. Ahpra has interim guidance on financial conflicts of interest (secondary coverage only). Seek legal advice before taking a fee tied to referrals of registered practitioners.
9. Privacy Act: a business that provides a health service and holds health information is covered even if small (https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/opting-in-to-the-privacy-act and OAIC health pages). Whether a directory counts as a "health service provider" is UNSURE.
10. No reliable public benchmark exists for enquiry-to-booking conversion in directories. Only vendor blogs, with unsourced numbers.

## (c) Fee families and trade-offs

- **Flat subscription listing** (Psychology Today, GoodTherapy). Pros: predictable, simple, needs no payments, no leakage issue. Cons: practitioners pay before getting any client. Fairness complaints arise when referrals fall (reports of 50% drops and "zero referrals" for some therapists: https://clearhealthcosts.com/blog/2025/12/therapists-say-psychology-today-referrals-have-dried-up-and-express-concern/, anecdotal). Suits: platforms with traffic already, or trust-first brands that avoid taking a share of healing income.
- **Pay per lead** (Thumbtack, Bark). Pros: fee triggered by an actual enquiry, which suits your current enquiry-only flow. Cons: practitioners pay whether or not they win the client; can feel extractive and invite lead-quality disputes. In health and healing, "selling" a seeker's enquiry may feel ethically uncomfortable. Credits and refund guarantees are used to soften this.
- **Commission on first booking only** (Fresha, Mindbody). Pros: aligned with the platform's real contribution (discovery); avoids charging for loyal repeat clients; reduces the incentive to leave. Cons: needs a way to attribute and record bookings; minimums distort low prices; "was this client already mine" disputes (Mindbody handles this with record matching and own-link exemptions).
- **Commission on all bookings** (Airbnb-style). Pros: larger revenue per client. Cons: strongest leakage incentive for repeat-relationship services like therapy; requires payments and ongoing value delivery. Evidence in (d).
- **Freemium plus paid tools** (Fresha software, Insight Timer). Pros: low barrier, grows supply. Cons: needs product depth (scheduling, payments, marketing tools) that a pre-launch directory lacks.
- **Sponsored or featured placement.** Pros: optional, uncorrelated with outcomes for the seeker. Cons: in health and healing it can look like buying trust. Must be clearly labelled (see (e)).
- **Donation or pay-what-you-can** (Insight Timer donations). Pros: fits values-led brand; low friction. Cons: small and unpredictable revenue; third-party store cuts apply to in-app items.
- **Introduction fee.** A one-off fee when an enquiry converts to a first session. This is the same economics as first-booking commission but is harder to verify without payments, so it relies on practitioner self-reporting. I found no published example in wellness directories (UNSURE whether any exist).

## (d) Leakage evidence and mitigations

- A study on a ride and delivery-type market found introducing commissions raised leakage by nearly 4 percentage points, roughly doubling detected leakage (https://english.phbs.pku.edu.cn/info/3882/48882.htm). Same paper stream cites a Harvard case estimating about 90% of freelance-marketplace jobs start on-platform and finish offline (secondary; UNSURE).
- A contrasting paper on a home-cleaning platform found weak evidence of disintermediation (claims at most about 1 in 83 relationships; https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/bray/doc/disintermediation/disintermediation.pdf). Context: cleaning is lower trust and more interchangeable than therapy, so may not transfer.
- Industry guidance (Sharetribe) lists insurance, payment routing, reputation tied to platform transactions, provider tools, and contact filtering. It concedes contact filtering is easy to bypass and can backfire, and gives almost no measured effects (https://www.sharetribe.com/academy/how-to-discourage-people-from-going-around-your-payment-system/).
- Mindbody and Fresha sidestep leakage by charging only once, and by not charging when the client came via the business's own link (Mindbody page above). That design accepts leakage after the first booking as normal.
- I found no study isolating first-booking-only fees versus all-bookings fees. Evidence on "what works" is mostly practitioner opinion.
- Therapy-specific note: relationships often run for many sessions, and the platform's discovery value is front-loaded. This points toward a first-contact or first-session fee, or a flat fee, rather than ongoing commission (my inference, not a sourced finding).

## (f) Benchmarks found

- Take rates: Mindbody 20% capped at USD 30; Fresha about 20% with minimum; Treatwell 10 to 35% (conflicting); a UK therapy platform 15% first session then 5%; Airbnb about 15.5% (disputed). Sources in (b).
- Enquiry-to-booking: no authoritative directory benchmark. Vendor blogs suggest under 30 to 50% is weak, or 15 to 28% without a follow-up process, none with stated methodology (https://ainora.lt/blog/therapy-intake-conversion-statistics-2026). A medical-practice call benchmark of 60.4% exists (https://www.liine.com/wp-content/uploads/dlm_uploads/2024/02/Specialty-Healthcare-Marketing-Benchmark-Report-Q1-2024.pdf), but it is not therapy.
- Reply speed is repeatedly named as the main driver (same vendor blogs). Treat as plausible, not proven.
